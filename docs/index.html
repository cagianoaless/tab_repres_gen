Graph-to-Table Interfaces Control Relational Generalisation

Learning on Graphs 2026 · Accepted oral presentation

Alessandro Cagiano1 Lorenzo Molfetta2 Gianluca Moro2 Matteo Zignani1 Pasquale Minervini3,4

1 University of Milan 2 University of Bologna 3 University of Edinburgh 4 Miniml.AI

Paper PDF Code Reproduce

Abstract

Graph-to-table systems combine three design choices: an encoder that maps relational evidence to feature rows, a context constructor that selects labelled examples, and a tabular decoder. We investigate whether these components can be evaluated independently through matched comparisons that change one component while holding the rest of the pipeline and the evaluation constant. Across logistic regression, CATBOOST, and TABPFN-3 on CLUTRR, 57 GraphLog worlds, and 57 knowledge graphs, we show that conclusions about one component can change when the others change. On CLUTRR, absolute positional encodings maintain all distinctions among the evaluated queries and fit the short-path setting nearly perfectly; yet, they generalise worse than relation-count features because the supported prefix acts as a shortcut that breaks down on longer paths. On GraphLog, encoder preference depends on the decoder, while the relation-count representation degrades as CatBoost tree depth increases. Across different knowledge graphs, adding the frozen ULTRA score to local features is consistently useful, whereas adding local features to the score has a smaller and less stable effect. A pointwise TABPFN-3 readout using the full encoder underperforms native ULTRA on 48 of 57 datasets. These results show that encoder assessment depends jointly on context and decoder, while panel-level conclusions can also depend on evaluation and aggregation protocols.

What the experiments isolate

A graph-to-table system turns a relational query into a feature row, selects labelled examples, and asks a tabular model to predict. An end-to-end score mixes all three choices. A representation can retain the information needed for a task while exposing patterns that the decoder cannot learn from its available context.

We use matched comparisons that change one component at a time. Encoder comparisons share evaluation queries and context examples. Decoder comparisons share the same feature values before decoder-specific categorical encoding. Context interventions keep the encoder and evaluation fixed. The experiments cover short-to-long path transfer on CLUTRR and GraphLog, followed by candidate ranking across 57 knowledge graphs.

We also check what the rows can distinguish before fitting a decoder. If differently labelled queries collapse to the same row, a row-only predictor cannot separate them. But a perfect empirical ceiling can simply mean that every row is unique. It does not establish that a decoder can infer a transferable rule. The CLUTRR results make this distinction concrete.

Results

A supported prefix can obstruct length transfer

CLUTRR tests transfer from context paths of lengths 2–3 to evaluation paths of lengths 4–10. Absolute positions retain the relation at each position; relation counts discard that ordering. At 80 context examples per relation, TabPFN-3 reaches 28.9% accuracy with relation counts, versus 10.3% with absolute positions, even though every evaluated absolute-position row is unique. Accuracy is averaged equally over evaluation lengths 4–10, then over three paired context seeds.

To identify what causes the drop, we append a positional block to the count features and permute whole blocks among paths of the same length. Restoring the correct alignment keeps the block's dimensionality and collection of values unchanged, but costs TabPFN-3 another 10.4 percentage points. The harmful signal comes from how the positional features align with the query.

The aligned encoders already fit 99.0–99.5% of the labelled context. Zeroing positions beyond the supported length barely changes predictions. Together, these controls support a shortcut explanation: the decoder learns a highly predictive short prefix that fails on longer paths.

Broader context helps locally. Replacing 20 additional short paths with 20 length-4 paths, at the same total budget, improves TabPFN-3 by 75.8 points at length 4, but only 3.3 points at lengths 5–10. A shared-parameter recurrent model reaches 70.5% overall accuracy at the largest budget, showing that substantially stronger transfer is attainable on this split.

Encoder preference depends on the decoder

GraphLog's 57 worlds share a vocabulary of 20 directed relations while changing the relational rules. This fixes feature dimensionality and coordinate meaning across worlds. With the context and evaluation matched, relation counts beat absolute positions for logistic regression, but lose for CatBoost and TabPFN-3. The mean differences are +0.065, −0.157 and −0.079 in macro balanced accuracy.

This reversal is not explained by a wholesale loss of label distinctions. Both encoders retain high class-balanced ceilings. Yet the count representation is at or below each world's chance level in 30 of 57 worlds for CatBoost and 12 for TabPFN-3, compared with 1 and 4 worlds for absolute positions.

A CatBoost depth sweep exposes the failure more clearly. From depth 1 to 7, context macro balanced accuracy for relation counts rises from 83.0% to 94.8%, while evaluation macro balanced accuracy falls from 0.201 to 0.125. Both average equally over worlds; evaluation first averages equally over lengths 4–5. Absolute-position performance stays comparatively stable. Better context fit accompanies worse transfer for the count representation.

The dihedral control in Figure 3 also prevents a blanket preference for coarser features. Here, coarsening removes sequence order and merges task-relevant identities. Performance stays near chance. The useful representation depends on both which distinctions the task needs and how the decoder uses the available context.

The frozen score and local features contribute differently

In the knowledge-graph panel, each candidate triple becomes a row and the decoder ranks candidates for a missing head or tail entity. The Local encoder combines relation identity with endpoint, pair, path and community statistics. The Score encoder exposes only the score of the frozen graph-native ULTRA model.

Adding Score to Local is consistently useful. It improves equal-family mean reciprocal rank (MRR) by +0.048 to +0.129 across the three decoders and two negative-context constructions. Five of six contrasts survive joint correction. The reverse addition, Local to Score, ranges from −0.039 to +0.064, and none of those six contrasts survives correction. The two feature blocks make markedly different contributions.

The labelled negative context also changes performance. Replacing filtered-uniform negatives with structurally plausible negatives reduces MRR by 0.020 to 0.122 across all nine informative encoder–decoder combinations. The positive context and evaluation queries stay fixed, and negative selection uses graph-based criteria without consulting evaluation labels or the frozen score.

A separate matched control isolates rejection of known positives and finds much smaller effects. The main context contrast therefore reflects more than that filtering choice. Even with a fixed encoder, which labelled examples reach the decoder changes the measured result.

A tabular readout can lose a useful native ranking

The Full encoder combines Local, Global and the frozen ULTRA score. Yet TabPFN-3 with Full underperforms native ULTRA on 48 of 57 datasets and 14 of 16 provenance families. Including the upstream score in a richer row does not ensure that the learned readout preserves its candidate ordering.

The aggregate comparison changes with the weighting. Full-minus-native MRR is +0.006 with equal weight per provenance family, but −0.035 with equal weight per dataset. Excluding MetaFam yields −0.041 under equal-family aggregation. A positive family average can coexist with losses on most datasets.

A sanity check distinguishes reranking from changing the score scale. Score-only logistic regression preserves native ULTRA ranks exactly across all 684 dataset–budget–seed combinations. Adding global topological features to Local plus Score changes TabPFN-3 MRR by only +0.005, with sensitivity interval [−0.001,+0.010] and no support after joint correction. The native ranking is an essential reference whenever that score enters the table.

Different queries need different graph evidence

Aggregate MRR hides a second distinction. Among 7,296 directional queries, 1,622 have an observed direct or reverse edge of another relation between their endpoints. The other 5,674 queries lack such other-relation pair evidence. This connectivity is measured in the support graph, excluding the target relation.

Features of the endpoint pair, called Dyadic, outperform bounded-path features in each endpoint-connected group. The ordering reverses when other-relation pair evidence is absent. Within that group, equal-family MRR across decoders is only 0.001 to 0.002 for Dyadic, compared with 0.045 to 0.052 for Paths and 0.043 to 0.094 for the broader Local encoder. These conditional means are not pooled over the query counts.

This explains why a feature set that works well on connected pairs can be inadequate for most queries. Reporting the query mix is part of explaining the encoder's performance. The stratified comparisons use the same queries, candidate sets and evaluation protocol, so the differences are visible without changing the ranking task.

Further results from the appendix

The appendix tests the alignment control on generated tasks and examines why knowledge graphs differ in ranking difficulty. These results complement the native-ranking and query-connectivity analyses above.

The alignment control recovers known relational evidence

We generate tasks governed by reciprocity, typed composition, structural roles and shared community membership, with disjoint context and evaluation graphs. Correctly aligned evidence becomes more useful as the labelled context grows. Jointly permuting complete feature rows preserves the block's dimensions and covariance but breaks its correspondence to each query. Under this permutation, ROC-AUC stays between 0.497 and 0.508, close to chance.

Context separation and candidate count track shared difficulty

A simple context-based heuristic combines the strongest single-column separation of positive and negative examples with the size of the candidate pool. Without refitting, it orders observed TabPFN-3 mean rank with Spearman correlation 0.957 across 455 dataset–encoder strata at 200 examples per class. The same broad relationship appears for logistic regression and CatBoost.

This is an exploratory description of shared difficulty, not a validated rule for choosing a decoder. Leave-one-family-out selectors do not improve on their in-fold majority baseline.

One frozen score is often the strongest individual column

The frozen ULTRA score is the strongest single-column context separator on 55 of 57 knowledge graphs. Most constructed local and global columns lie closer to chance. This helps explain why adding the score to local features is consistently useful, while leaving room for multivariate and query-conditional effects that a one-column diagnostic cannot measure.

How to compare graph-to-table systems

  • Separate retained information from learned transfer. Report row collisions and singleton mass, then test alignment and context support. A perfect row ceiling and near-perfect context fit can accompany poor extrapolation.
  • Cross the encoder with the decoder and context. GraphLog changes the preferred encoder across decoders; the knowledge-graph experiments change performance through the negative context alone.
  • Keep native rankings and aggregation visible. When importing a pretrained graph score, compare against its original ranking. Show dataset and family summaries alongside query-connectivity strata.

The knowledge-graph panel is fixed. Its bootstrap intervals describe sensitivity to the composition of that panel, and leave-one-family-out checks expose reliance on individual families. They are not population-level confidence intervals for all knowledge graphs.
